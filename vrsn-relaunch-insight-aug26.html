Priority finding

About half of site visitors are not being counted

While reviewing performance we found a measurement issue on get.vrsn.co: the Meta and Google tracking tags fire later than they should. They fire only once the entire page has finished loading, rather than when it becomes visible. A visitor who arrives, reads, and leaves before that moment is never recorded.

Right now, only about 45% of paid clicks are being counted as site visits. Mehdi has reviewed this with us and reproduced the same figure independently, so it is confirmed on both sides.

Why this matters, and what happens next

Meta decides who to show ads to based on the visits it can see. With roughly half of them missing, it has been working from an incomplete picture, which limits how well every campaign can perform, on both accounts.

The fix is small and is our first priority this week. Once it is live, reported traffic will rise noticeably. That jump is recovered measurement, not new demand. We flag it now so the step change is read correctly when it appears.

A related, smaller issue: checkout events are also under-counting. We are correcting both in the same pass.

Our recommendation

Hold price here. The reduction worked because cost per order had room to fall. A further reduction would take basket size down again from an already lower base, and there is no evidence in the data that acquisition cost has another third in it. We would rather grow basket size than cut price again.

What is working

The pattern behind the two best campaigns

The strongest performers share one construction: the ad names a specific person, states the offer, and carries the guarantee, then sends that person to a page that repeats the same promise. Where that chain is intact, return is above 2.0. Where the ad describes a symptom instead of a person, it is not.

Nurse: 2.10 campaign ROAS "100 days. About 25 shifts to decide." Speaks to a nurse in her own language, shows the product, states the offer and the guarantee together. The strongest cart rate in the account by a wide margin.
Barefoot: 2.73 ad ROAS "The pain was never the movement." Resolves a tension the reader already holds, then demonstrates the mechanism. Our single best-performing ad, and still improving as it scales.
How we are applying it

This construction has only ever run on two of the seven campaigns. Extending it to the others is the clearest near-term opportunity in the account and requires no additional budget, only new executions of a format we have already proven.

The people behind the numbers

Reading the age, gender and campaign data together, three buyer profiles account for nearly all orders:

The professional on her feet

Woman, 45 to 64, works a standing job. Nurses are the proven case; teachers, retail and hospitality are the same person in a different uniform.

  • Responds to ads that name her job and state the offer plainly.
  • Converts at the highest rate in the account once she reaches the page.
  • Buys on the guarantee: 100 days measured in shifts, not days.

The active retiree

Woman, 65+, protective of her independence. Over half of all orders. She is not buying a shoe, she is buying the walks, travel and grandchildren time that foot pain has been taking away.

  • Strongest on Nurse (2.57) and Travel (2.15).
  • Responds to identity and reassurance, not to symptom imagery.
  • The buyer our landing page revisions are being written for.

The considered upgrader

Woman, 55 to 64, still working or newly retired. The most efficient buyer we have: cheapest to acquire and the best return.

  • Compares options before buying; the trial and free returns close her.
  • Most likely to take the insole add-on and the second pair, which is what lifts average order to 1.42× base price.
  • Currently under-served with a quarter of budget; dedicated delivery is planned in phase two.

Men are a smaller but real segment at 15% of orders and a 1.08 return. They buy through the same offer-led format rather than through their own campaigns, so we reach them by format, not by separate targeting.

Landing pages

Three opportunities on site

Match the narrator to the reader

The three long-form story pages share a template, a length and an offer. The variable that separates them is who narrates.

  • The page told by a female nurse returns 1.34.
  • The page told by a 38-year-old male runner returns 0.49.
  • The page told by a 47-year-old male hiker returns 0.21.

Given that 85% of buyers are 55 and over and predominantly women, aligning the narrator to that reader is a copy revision rather than a rebuild, and it is the highest-value change available on site.

Show one discount, consistently

A visitor arriving from an ad can currently encounter three different discount figures between the creative and the page they land on.

  • Story pages state up to 55% off.
  • Advertorial pages state 47% off.
  • The best-performing ad states up to 54% off.

Each may be accurate for a given basket, but the inconsistency arrives at the moment of purchase. Agreeing one canonical figure is a quick win.

Add a short offer page

Buyers who are already decided currently land on either a product page or an eight-minute article. Neither is built to close someone who has made up their mind.

  • A short page built around the 100-day trial: the guarantee, returns, sizing and a clear purchase path.
  • This also gives retargeting a proper destination, which it does not have today.

Vivobarefoot runs precisely this page for the same 100-day proposition, and it is the model we would follow.

Approach: each launches as a small, individually measurable pilot with a defined stop threshold, only after the measurement fix is live, so spend stays contained and the results are readable.

A note on pacing

We are deliberately holding budget flat until measurement is corrected. Scaling against an incomplete signal risks buying the wrong audience at volume. Once the fix is live and we have a clean week, we will have a properly evidenced basis for increasing spend.
