Six days live: what we learned and what changes now

Two campaigns make money. Two lose it. The creative is not the problem. We are sending the right people to pages that talk to someone else, and our tracking is missing half of them.

Decisions

Three things we are doing

1

Fix the tracking first

Our Meta pixel only counts a visitor after the whole page finishes loading. We are missing about half of them. Every decision so far was made on this broken number.

2

Turn off 65+ in two campaigns

Age 65+ makes money on Nurse (3.09) and Travel (2.85). It loses money on Barefoot (0.55) and /b (0.97). So we cut it there only, not everywhere.

1.46 → 1.62 · no extra spend

Change who tells the story on our pages

Our story pages are written by people aged 38 to 47. Most of our buyers are women over 55. The one page that matches its reader is the one that makes money.

What the data tells us

Three findings

01

We sell to people who already know they have a problem

2.64ROAS from ads aimed at people ready to buy
0.69ROAS from ads that try to make people notice the problem
$807spent on ads that got zero purchases — 23% of budget

So: we grow by adding new customer types — nurse, traveller, and so on — each with its own matching page. Not by making the audience bigger.

02

People buy when the ad shows someone like them

56%of visitors add to cart when the ad names a person and the offer
4.5%when the ad only shows sore feet
1.34 vs 0.21same page, told by a nurse vs told by a male hiker

So: one customer type = one ad + one page that matches it. Also ignore CTR — our highest-click ads sell the least.

03

The offer is fine. How we buy traffic is not.

$101.58average order on a $69 product — people buy 2 pairs or add insoles
5 of 7campaigns put the biggest budget on the wrong ad
45%of clicks are counted as reaching the page

So: do not discount more. The money is lost in how traffic is bought and measured, not in the price.

The pattern

The two that sell both show the product and give a reason to act now. The two that fail are beautiful and empty — they earn the click and then ask the viewer to do the work. That is the whole of finding 02, in four pictures.

Blocker

The pixel fires too late, on every page

  • All 7 pages start the Meta pixel inside a load listener.
  • load does not mean "page is visible". It means "every file finished downloading".
  • Our pages send a 7 KB shell and then load a 1.14 MB JavaScript file.
  • So anyone who leaves before that finishes is never counted, even though they saw the page.
<script defer>
  window.addEventListener('load', function() {     // <- remove this wrapper
    ... fbevents.js ...
    fbq('init', '582450040718073');
    fbq('track', 'PageView');
  });
</script>

The fix: start the pixel and GA4 in the page head so they fire straight away. Same on all 7 pages, plus /adv/higher-load and /adv/pregnancy before those go live.

We can measure the damage today, without deploying anything

Microsoft Clarity is already installed and is not affected. It fires at 309ms. The Meta pixel fires at 863ms on fast desktop, and much later on phones. Compare Clarity sessions to Meta landing page views for the same dates. The gap is what we are losing.

Three moves, in order

Do these one after the other, not at the same time. If we change everything at once we will not know what worked.

this week

MOVE 02

Put money behind what works

this week, after move 01

Landing pages · for Mehdi and Neil

Our story pages tell the wrong person's story

I read all three story pages. They use the same template, same length, same offer, even the same emotional beats. The only real difference is who is telling the story. That difference alone explains the results.

The clearest example

We spent $353 sending women aged 65 and over to an 8-minute story written by a 47-year-old man about hiking and golf. He writes "I felt old. I'm 47." Our reader is 70. Only 3.7 out of 100 visitors added to cart, against 10 out of 100 everywhere else.

The angle is good. The person telling it is wrong. This is a rewrite of the narrator, not of the idea.

Fix 1 — Foot Pain: change the narrator

Keep the structure and the idea. The story is about losing things you used to do and accepting it. That is the same idea behind our best ad.

  • Narrator becomes a woman in her early 60s.
  • Change the activities. The right ones are already on the page — grandkids, farmers market, evening walks. Move them up. Cut hiking, golf and Patagonia.
  • Delete the line "I felt old. I'm 47."
  • Keep the daughter recommending it. That works well at this age.
Smallest change, biggest expected gain

Fix 2 — Orthotics: same problem

Daniel is 38 and runs. Our reader is 60+ and is not training for anything. The idea itself is good: "a brace does not make anything stronger."

  • Narrator becomes a woman 60+ who has worn custom orthotics for years and was told it is permanent.
  • Keep the cost detail ($380, insurance paid half). Price hits hard with this buyer.
  • Remove the running and soccer coaching.
Page is paused, so no rush. Still cheap to do.

The test that teaches us the most

Also — our live pages load slower than the unused ones

The two unused /adv/ pages send the full page from the server, so text appears immediately. Every page we currently spend on sends an empty 7 KB shell and builds itself with JavaScript.

  • This matters most for 55+ buyers, who tend to use older phones.
  • It also makes the pixel problem worse, because our tracking waits for that build to finish.
  • Use the /adv/ setup for anything new.
Bigger job. Schedule it, do not block on it.

Also — we show three different discounts

The story pages say "up to 55% off". The /adv/ pages say "47% off". Our best-performing ad says "up to 54% off". All three may be true depending on cart size, but a visitor sees a different number on the ad than on the page.

  • Decide which number is correct and use it everywhere.
  • The /adv/ pages also use a different font (Hanken Grotesk) from the main site (Silka and Inter).
Small, but it costs trust at the moment of buying.
Landing pages · new test

A simple offer page, for retargeting

What it isOne short page. The offer, the 100-day guarantee, sizing help, and a buy button. Under a minute to read.
Who it is forPeople who already visited our site once and left without buying.
What it replacesNothing. Every page we have now keeps running exactly as it is.

Why test it

Retargeting has nowhere to send people

Sending someone back to the same page they already left will not change their mind. They did not leave because they missed information.

It answers what stopped them

Usually two things: "what if I don't like them" and "will they fit me". That is the guarantee and the sizing.

It is the cheapest page to build

No new photos, no new story, no long copy. Only the offer, the returns and an FAQ.

A page that already does this

Vivobarefoot sells barefoot shoes and also gives 100 days. Here is how their page is built, top to bottom.

vivobarefoot.com/us/100-day-trial
1

2
"Discover the feeling with our 100-day trial"The headline is the guarantee, not the shoe.
3
One paragraph of reassurance"Return any time within 100 days for a full refund, no questions asked (even if they're covered in mud)."
4

5

6

7

8

What is not on that page

No wall of testimonials. No long story. No founder story. Every FAQ question is about how to return the shoes — not about feet.

That is the model. The page removes the last reasons not to click buy.
